### FCC extends foreign-router ban lifeline to 2029

> **Summary.** The FCC *(Federal Communications Commission, US telecom regulator)* will allow existing foreign-made routers and drones to receive software updates through 2029, two years past the original deadline. This is a partial retreat from a national-security ban on equipment from companies like Huawei and DJI, which remains in place for new purchases. The waiver applies to patching, not new sales — a distinction that matters for the millions of devices already deployed.

The core tension here is between security theater and operational reality. The ban on new foreign networking gear was always going to strand a massive installed base; pretending those devices could run unpatched for years was a recipe for botnets, not safety. The FCC's extension acknowledges this without admitting the broader policy incoherence — we "secured" the supply chain by forbidding future purchases while ignoring the vulnerability surface we just enlarged. The framing as "slight relaxation" obscures that this was the only feasible move; the alternative was punishing users for compliance with a prior rule. What's strong: the reporting names the affected device categories (routers, drones) and the concrete timeline. What's thin: no estimate of how many devices are in scope, no discussion of whether manufacturers will actually continue support that long, and minimal scrutiny of whether 2029 is long enough for enterprise refresh cycles.

This reads as regulatory face-saving more than principled adjustment. The 2027 original deadline likely assumed faster hardware turnover than happens in practice, especially in rural and budget-constrained deployments. We'll want to watch whether vendors like TP-Link and DJI use this window to wind down support anyway — patch availability requires manufacturer cooperation, not just FCC permission.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/fcc-slightly-relaxes-foreign-router-ban-allows-software-updates-until-2029/))

### "Shai-Hulud" worm breaches 172 npm/PyPI packages, targets developer credential vaults

> **Summary.** A supply-chain attack has compromised 172 packages on npm *(Node.js package manager)* and PyPI *(Python Package Index)* since May 11, distributing a worm that harvests credentials from over 100 file paths including AWS *(Amazon Web Services, cloud platform)* keys, SSH *(Secure Shell, encrypted remote access protocol)* private keys, and — for the first time in this threat actor's campaigns — password manager databases for 1Password and Bitwarden. The worm, dubbed "Shai-Hulud" by researchers, also steals tokens for AI coding agents Claude and Kiro. Any development environment that imported these packages should be treated as fully compromised.

This is the most consequential security story this cycle because of the credential scope and the novel password-manager targeting. The attack chain is well-documented: malicious packages with valid provenance (suggesting compromised maintainer accounts or build pipelines) propagate through routine developer installs. What's strong is the specificity — 172 named packages, 100+ credential paths, named tools. What's thin: the "valid provenance" claim needs scrutiny; "valid" could mean signed but not verified, or signed with stolen keys. The article doesn't distinguish, and the distinction matters for whether CI/CD *(Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment, automated build pipelines)* audit steps would catch this.

The password-manager targeting is the signal to watch. Previously these campaigns stopped at browser cookies and plaintext configs; going after 1Password and Bitwarden databases suggests either new exploitation techniques or simply that developers are storing these files in predictable locations. Either way, it raises the recovery cost dramatically — rotating API keys is automation; rotating personal password vaults is manual, error-prone, and often incomplete. The "6 actionable steps" framing in the headline is vendor-content marketing dressed as reporting; the actual mitigation is more brutal: burn down affected environments and rebuild from known-clean state.

([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/security/shai-hulud-worm-172-npm-pypi-packages-valid-provenance-ci-cd-audit))

### Twin brothers wiped 96 government databases minutes post-firing in classic access-control failure

> **Summary.** Two twin brothers, fired from IT roles at an unnamed government entity, deleted 96 databases approximately ten minutes after their termination — because their credentials had not yet been revoked. The case is being prosecuted as intentional destruction; the brothers had reportedly warned management that their simultaneous firing created a security risk.

This is less a hacking story than an organizational competence story, which makes it the right kind of infrastructure item. The technical details are almost incidental: they used standard admin access, not exploits. The indictment apparently includes logged queries like "how to delete database" run after the firing meeting, which is damning for intent but also darkly comic in its amateurism. What's strong: the timing (10 minutes), the scale (96 databases), the prosecutorial evidence. What's thin: which government entity remains unnamed, whether backups existed and were tested, and what the actual recovery cost was — the article mentions "millions" but doesn't attribute the estimate.

The framing as "case study" is apt. The brothers' reported warning that simultaneous firing was risky suggests either genuine process concern used as cover, or a genuinely dysfunctional organization that ignored basic offboarding. Either fits the pattern we see in public-sector IT: credential management is boring, underfunded, and politically unrewarding until it catastrophically isn't. The ten-minute gap is the number that should haunt CIOs — revocation latency measured in hours is common industry-wide.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/drop-database-what-not-to-do-after-losing-an-it-job/))
