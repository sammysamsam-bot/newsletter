### Anthropic ships Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5, with a quiet self-preservation clause

> **Summary.** Anthropic released two models: *Claude Mythos 5* *(frontier research-grade model, strongest capability tier)* and *Claude Fable 5* *(same weights with strict safety guardrails for general use)*. Simon Willison's testing confirms Fable 5 is "slow, expensive, and quite happily churning through everything" thrown at it. A buried system-card detail drew sharper attention: Anthropic now limits Claude's effectiveness on requests targeting frontier *LLM* *(large language model)* development — pretraining pipelines, distributed training infrastructure, *ML* *(machine learning)* accelerator design — and won't notify users when the limiter triggers.

The dual-release strategy is a genuine experiment in shipping the same capabilities with different policy envelopes. Willison's first-party testing is the most useful early signal we have; his observation that "the challenge is finding tasks it can't do" is notable given his skeptical baseline. The self-limitation on AI development assistance is the story under the story. Anthropic frames it as a safety intervention against model self-improvement — but the mechanism is deliberately invisible ("you'll never know" per Willison's reading of the 319-page system card). That's a design choice worth interrogating: a refusal you can audit is one thing; silent degradation of helpfulness on certain technical topics is another. We don't know the false-positive rate or whether this applies to legitimate educational queries versus weaponized capability extraction.

*Andrej Karpathy* *(OpenAI founding member, former Tesla AI director)* captured the broader mood in a quoted take on Fable 5: the *Jevons paradox* *(economic phenomenon where increased efficiency raises total consumption)* is kicking in for software, with bespoke single-use apps and hyper-specific tools proliferating. The industrial-policy context matters too — OpenAI dropped an S-1 filing and two policy posts in the same window, making Anthropic's actual model release the outlier in a week otherwise heavy on positioning. Whether Fable 5's guardrails hold under red-team pressure, or quietly frustrate researchers who hit invisible walls, is the test to watch.

### Google DeepMind ships Gemma 4 12B and Gemini 3.5 Live Translate, doubling down on open-weights + voice

> **Summary.** Google released *Gemma 4 12B* *(unified encoder-free multimodal model in the open-weights Gemma family)* alongside *Gemini 3.5 Live Translate* *(near real-time speech translation rolling out to Google AI Studio, Translate, and Meet)*. The Gemma 4 architecture drops the separate vision encoder, which matters for inference efficiency; Live Translate targets fluid, natural-sounding voice conversion rather than text-pivot translation.

The encoder-free design in Gemma 4 is the technical bet worth tracking. Most multimodal models bolt a vision encoder onto a language backbone; unifying the architecture typically trades research elegance for practical fragility. DeepMind's claim here needs third-party replication to evaluate — the open-weights release helps, since Ollama and Hugging Face downstreams will stress-test it quickly. The Live Translate rollout is more clearly shipping product, and the "natural" qualifier is doing specific work: Google is pitching prosody and turn-taking preservation, not just accuracy. The deep integration into Meet and Translate suggests this is a consumer-scale deployment test, not a research demo. Whether it handles code-switching (the *ASR* *(automatic speech recognition)* benchmark Hugging Face published this week) gracefully is the edge case that breaks most voice systems.

([DeepMind Gemma 4](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-gemma-4-12b-a-unified-encoder-free-multimodal-model/) · [DeepMind Live Translate](https://deepmind.google/blog/fluid-natural-voice-translation-with-gemini-35-live-translate/))

### OpenAI clusters: Codex customer posts, S-1 filing, and policy positioning

The Codex stories emphasize cross-platform investigation and "one-shot" spec generation; the S-1 has no disclosed timeline.

Clustering these because the policy posts and S-1 are clearly pre-IPO positioning, not operational news. That said, both are OpenAI-sourced and uncorroborated; "one-shot specs" is a claim that depends entirely on what "spec" means in context. The S-1 filing matters as an event — OpenAI is formally preparing for public markets — but the confidential draft tells us little until the registration statement goes public. The industrial policy post is standard CEO-letter fare; the Economic Research Exchange launch is slightly more concrete, opening applications for outside research on AI's labor-market effects.

### xAI's Grok powers Gopuff's "Go agent" — another frontier-model retail integration

> **Summary.** *xAI* *(Elon Musk's AI lab, Grok developer)* collaborated with *Gopuff* *(instant delivery platform)* to launch the Go agent, a personal shopping assistant using frontier text, image, and audio models. The agent handles product discovery, ordering, and delivery coordination.

The retail-agent integration space is crowded — Instacart, DoorDash, and Amazon have all announced similar experiments. xAI's involvement is notable because Grok has been positioned as a "rebellious" or "anti-woke" alternative, and this is straightforward commerce infrastructure. The claim that the agent "knows what you want" is doing heavy lifting; whether that's collaborative filtering, explicit preference learning, or LLM-based inference from chat history determines both utility and privacy implications. The post is thin on technical detail — no benchmark, no user-volume metrics, no comparison to non-Grok baselines. Treat this as a partnership announcement, not evidence of Grok's retail superiority.
