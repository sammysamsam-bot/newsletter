### RFK Jr. launches psychiatric drug reduction plan

> **Summary.** Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced federal steps to reduce antidepressant and psychiatric medication prescribing, framing it as combating "overmedicalization." The plan emphasizes alternative treatments and supported discontinuation. Details remain sparse on implementation scope and clinical protocols.

Kennedy's framing—"overmedicalization"—is doing substantial ideological lifting here. The available coverage from AllSides aggregates government statements without independent verification of what specifically HHS will change: Medicaid reimbursement rules? FDA guidance? CDC prescribing recommendations? The claim that America is overmedicated has some empirical support—antidepressant prescribing rates did climb 40%+ over the past two decades—but the jump from that observation to policy specifics matters enormously. Will this mean expanded access to talk therapy and psychiatric nurse practitioners, or primarily medication cessation campaigns? The Kennedy family history with mental health policy (his uncle's Community Mental Health Act, the subsequent deinstitutionalization disasters) should make observers cautious about grand rhetoric outpacing service infrastructure.

What's thin: any mechanism for safe tapering. Psychiatric medication discontinuation, particularly for benzodiazepines and some antidepressants, carries substantial withdrawal risks and suicidality spikes when mishandled. The announcement's silence on how HHS will train providers or monitor outcomes is conspicuous. Stronger coverage would press Kennedy's team on whether they're funding replacement services commensurate with any prescribing reduction, or repeating the pattern of dismantling without rebuilding.

### North Korea writes unification out of its constitution

> **Summary.** Pyongyang has formally removed all reunification references from its constitution, adding territorial definitions and explicit Kim Jong Un authority over nuclear weapons. The revision codifies Kim's "two hostile states" doctrine treating South Korea as permanent adversary rather than eventual kin.

This is constitutional architecture catching up to rhetoric. Kim Jong Un declared South Korea the "principal foe" in early 2024; now the foundational document reflects it. The move carries practical implications: North Korea's previous constitutional claims to the entire peninsula complicated its international legal status and provided nominal justification for restraint in cross-border provocations. A defined territorial boundary, however fictive in practice, signals Pyongyang may calculate that clearer statehood claims yield diplomatic leverage—perhaps seeking recognition pathways or at least complicating South Korea's legal arguments against its nuclear status.

The sourcing here relies on South Korean government document review and expert interpretation rather than independent verification. Oxford's Edward Howell is appropriately cautious: this "perception is now permanent" in North Korean eyes, but perceptions can be strategic performances too. What's unaddressed in available coverage is whether constitutional change alters military command structures or crisis communication protocols between the Koreas. The "normal state" framing some experts float—reducing ideological verbiage—is plausible but speculative; Kim's nuclear weapons clause suggests exceptionalism, not normalization, remains the operative concept.

### Indiana GOP primary extends Trump's intra-party discipline campaign

The Dispatch's analysis of the Indiana State Senate primary—where Trump-backed challenger defeated incumbent Travis Holdman, who had opposed the President's redistricting plan—reads the result correctly as pyrrhic victory. Trump retains sufficient command of Republican primary electorates to punish procedural dissent. The "hostage crisis" framing captures something real: elected Republicans now weigh every vote against the certainty of primary challenge, not merely probability.

Where the analysis earns its keep is noting the downstream dysfunction. Legislative bodies require members who occasionally defy leadership for institutional reasons—redistricting independence being a paradigmatic case. Eliminating this capacity doesn't produce more loyal governance; it produces legislators who avoid hard votes or exit for lobbying careers. The Dispatch's center-right vantage here is useful precisely because it doesn't celebrate Trump's dominance as simple partisan success. It treats party institutional health as separable from electoral winning, a distinction increasingly rare in movement commentary.

Thin in the coverage: whether Holdman's defeat alters any specific 2026 legislative prospects, or if Indiana's GOP supermajority renders individual member independence moot regardless. The broader pattern—Trump's endorsement record versus his policy implementation record—deserves more quantitative attention than it typically receives. Winning primaries and winning governable majorities are different metrics.
