### The Meniscus Surgery Reckoning

> **Summary.** A decade-long Finnish trial found that arthroscopic partial meniscectomy — trimming torn knee cartilage — offered no benefit over sham surgery, with patients who got the real procedure actually doing worse at follow-up: more symptoms, faster osteoarthritis progression, and higher rates of reoperation. The study lands on roughly two million annual procedures worldwide.

This is practice-changing in a field that resisted change. The FIDELITY trial randomized 146 patients with knee pain and a degenerative meniscal tear to either real or sham surgery, then tracked them for ten years. The sham involved arthroscopy and manipulation without touching the meniscus — ethically defensible because prior trials already showed no mechanical advantage, yet the procedure persisted because surgeons and patients *believed* it worked. What's striking is the harm signal: the real-surgery group didn't merely fail to improve; they deteriorated faster. The mechanism likely involves altered joint biomechanics and accelerated cartilage wear after meniscal loss. The evidence quality is high — randomized, blinded, long-term — though the sample is modest for detecting rare complications.

The resistance will be institutional. Meniscectomy is revenue-generative, technically straightforward, and patient demand remains high. But the trajectory here resembles arthroscopy for osteoarthritis, which similarly collapsed after sham-controlled trials. Finnish orthopedics, where this trial originated, has already moved toward conservative management; whether that spreads to fee-for-service systems is the open question.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260505234603.htm) · [BMJ FIDELITY publication](https://www.bmj.com/content/383/bmj-2023-076332))

### A Nine-Week Immunotherapy Shot for Colorectal Cancer

> **Summary.** A UK-led trial of pembrolizumab *(anti-PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor)* given for just nine weeks before surgery in mismatch repair-deficient colorectal cancer produced disease-free survival rates near 100% at three years, potentially replacing months of postoperative chemotherapy. The NEOTORCH protocol treated 91 patients with neoadjuvant pembrolizumab alone.

The framing here matters. "Cancer-free for nearly 3 years" is the ScienceDaily headline; the Lancet Oncology paper reports no relapses at median follow-up of 35 months. The patient population is small but precisely selected: dMMR/MSI-high tumors, which are immunogenic and already responsive to PD-1 blockade. What shifts practice is the *sequence* — immunotherapy first, surgery potentially curative without further systemic treatment — and the brevity. Standard of care is surgery followed by 3-6 months of chemotherapy, with considerable toxicity.

The evidence is phase 2, single-arm, with no direct comparator. Historical controls suggest this outperforms, but we lack randomized data against standard neoadjuvant chemo or against pembrolizumab given postoperatively. The biological rationale is sound: early immune activation against intact tumor antigens, plus the possibility of complete pathological response obviating surgery entirely (some patients in this trial achieved this, though all underwent resection). What's thin is generalizability to the broader colorectal population — dMMR represents 15% of cases — and durability beyond three years.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260505234618.htm) · [Lancet Oncology](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(25)00147-0/fulltext))

### Inside the Lightning Machine

> **Summary.** Physicists are assembling a coherent picture of thunderstorm electrodynamics through coordinated instrument networks: *LOFAR* *(Dutch radio telescope array, repurposed for lightning detection)*, *EUCLID* *(European lightning detection network)*, and airborne sensors, revealing that storms generate energetic electron avalanches, gamma-ray flashes, and kilometer-scale plasma structures far more violent than classic models assumed.

The Quanta piece synthesizes several converging research threads, and the framing — "the answer keeps getting more interesting" — is earned rather than merely enthusiastic. The old picture involved charge separation via graupel-ice collisions, producing a simple dipole that discharged through stepped leaders. What's emerged is a plasma physics problem: relativistic electron runaway, *Terrestrial Gamma-Ray Flashes* *(TGFs, first confirmed in 1994)*, and the discovery that lightning channels are surrounded by "streamer coronas" — filamentary plasma networks visible only in radio interferometry. The LOFAR data are particularly crisp, mapping lightning initiation at meter-scale resolution and catching upward negative leaders that precede many cloud-to-ground strikes.

What's still incomplete is the causal chain. We see correlated phenomena — gamma flashes, radio bursts, optical emissions — but the sequence and physical coupling remain contested. The "interesting" quality is genuine uncertainty: whether runaway electrons trigger breakdown or merely accompany it, whether the observed structures are necessary or incidental to the main discharge. The methodology shift is as significant as any single finding. Lightning was historically instrumented by sparse ground networks; treating storms as laboratory plasmas with distributed sensor webs is enabling physics that was previously inaccessible.

([Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/what-causes-lightning-the-answer-keeps-getting-more-interesting-20260506/))
