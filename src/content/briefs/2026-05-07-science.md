### Colon cancer immunotherapy shows durable remission with short pre-surgical course

> **Summary.** A UK-led trial found nine weeks of *pembrolizumab* *(PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor, Merck)* before surgery kept patients with mismatch-repair-deficient colorectal cancer cancer-free for nearly three years. This challenges the standard sequence of surgery followed by months of chemotherapy. The benefit appears confined to this molecular subtype, which comprises 15–20% of cases.

The evidence here is strong: randomized phase II data with median follow-up approaching three years, and the control arm (surgery-first) showed the expected recurrence pattern. The framing as "breakthrough" is earned for this specific subset, but the headline risk is overgeneralization. The trial's *neoadjuvant* *(pre-surgical)* approach lets researchers assess pathological complete response—a proxy that here predicted durable remission, though longer follow-up remains essential. The real shift would be practice-changing if confirmed in phase III: front-loading immunotherapy, sparing some patients chemotherapy toxicity entirely. What's thin is biomarker precision beyond the binary mismatch-repair status; we don't yet know which patients within this subset can safely omit surgery or further therapy.

The comparison to existing standards matters. Current *adjuvant* *(post-surgical)* pembrolizumab is already approved for stage III disease, but requires months of infusion after recovery from surgery. The neoadjuvant timing may exploit intact tumor antigen presentation and a less immunosuppressive postsurgical milieu—plausible mechanistically, though not proven superior head-to-head. The trial's size (~100 patients) limits subgroup analysis. Still, for a disease where chemotherapy buys modest benefit at substantial cost, this reordering warrants attention.

### Common knee meniscectomy fails trial against sham surgery, shows harm signal

> **Summary.** A decade-long Finnish trial of *arthroscopic partial meniscectomy* *(trimming torn knee cartilage)* found no pain benefit over placebo surgery, with operated patients showing faster osteoarthritis progression and higher reoperation rates. The procedure remains among the world's most common orthopedic surgeries.

This is practice-questioning evidence at scale: 146 patients randomized, 10-year follow-up, and a sham-controlled design that remains rare in surgery trials for ethical and logistical reasons. The harm signal—more symptoms, worse function, accelerated joint degeneration—was unexpected in magnitude and consistency. The framing is notable: the researchers don't hedge on clinical futility, and the osteoarthritis acceleration suggests structural damage from the procedure itself, not merely failed benefit. What's strong is the objective outcome data (radiographic progression, reoperation rates); what's harder to parse is pain reporting, which showed no group difference but carries placebo-response complexity.

The contextual gap is why this persists. Meniscectomy has survived prior skeptical trials (including the 2013 Finnish and 2016 Norwegian studies) because clinical practice adapts slowly, imaging findings drive referrals, and patients demand intervention. This trial's longer horizon and harm data may finally shift guidelines, though implementation will lag. The broader lesson—common procedures often lack rigorous efficacy evidence—applies well beyond orthopedics.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260505234603.htm))

### MIT find of "silent synapses" in adult cortex revises plasticity dogma

> **Summary.** *Silent synapses* *(AMPA-receptor-deficient connections, electrically inactive but structurally present)* were thought largely pruned after development; MIT researchers estimate 30% of adult cortical synapses fit this category, capable of rapid activation during learning. The finding used *expansion microscopy* *(tissue expansion for nanoscale resolution)* and electrophysiology in mouse visual cortex.

This reframes how we understand adult learning capacity. The prior developmental narrative held that silent synapses provide a substrate for early critical periods, then mature or disappear; finding them abundant in adults suggests the brain retains more raw material for circuit modification than assumed. The evidence pairing structural and functional assays is convincing—the *correlative* light microscopy validated with *patch-clamp* electrophysiology showing these synapses can be "unsilenced" by coincident activity. What's less clear is whether this population is functionally equivalent to developmental silent synapses or represents a distinct, perhaps degraded, state.

The methodological advance matters: expansion microscopy enabled synapse-scale census in intact tissue that electron microscopy destroys. The speculative extension to memory disorders—could silent synapse depletion explain age-related cognitive decline?—is plausible but unsupported by this data. The more grounded implication is for understanding why adult learning remains possible, and potentially for designing interventions that reactivate dormant connectivity rather than building new structure de novo.
