### Scientists May Have Found What Really Triggers Alzheimer's Disease

> **Summary.** A new theory argues that amyloid beta plaques aren't the root cause of Alzheimer's but rather an earlier step — they disrupt tau proteins that actually maintain neuron function. If the mechanism holds up, it could redirect drug development toward protecting tau rather than clearing plaques, though the finding needs replication in larger cohorts.

The Alzheimer's field has been stuck in a plaque-first paradigm for decades, with repeated failures of anti-amyloid drugs suggesting the framework was incomplete. This research proposes a sequential model: amyloid beta interferes with tau's normal role in stabilizing microtubules, and that disruption — not the plaques themselves — initiates the cascade of neurodegeneration. The framing is clearer than the evidence; "may have uncovered" and "could set off" signal that this is mechanistic work in cell or animal models, not a proven human pathway. What's strong is the conceptual shift: if tau protection becomes a viable target, it opens a genuinely different therapeutic avenue after billions spent on plaque clearance. What's thin is the distance from bench to bedside — the amyloid hypothesis also looked elegant in vitro before clinical translation repeatedly underwhelmed.

The real signal here is whether funding and trial pipelines pivot, or whether this joins the graveyard of beautiful Alzheimer's mechanisms that didn't survive human testing. We'd want to see independent replication and evidence that tau-stabilizing approaches show cognitive benefit in Phase II before calling this practice-changing.

### Ancient DNA Reveals Plague Was Already Killing Humans 5,500 Years Ago

> **Summary.** Hunter-gatherers in Siberia carried *Yersinia pestis* strains 3,000 years before cities or rats existed as plague amplifiers, with nearly 40% of individuals in studied cemeteries showing infection and family clusters suggesting epidemic spread. The finding dismantles the urban-rat model of plague emergence and points to social density among mobile populations as sufficient for transmission.

This is methodology-shifting more than practice-changing — it uses ancient DNA to recalibrate when and how plague became a human pathogen. The study sampled Siberian hunter-gatherer cemeteries and found *Y. pestis* in individuals who died 5,500 years ago, with phylogenetic placement showing these were genuine plague strains rather than ancestral environmental bacteria. The family burial clusters are particularly telling: multiple children and young adults dying together suggests person-to-person spread, not sporadic rodent exposure. That upends the textbook story where plague required urbanization, trade routes, and commensal rats to become epidemic.

The evidence quality hinges on sampling density and contamination controls, which the brief summary doesn't detail — we'd want to see whether the 40% prevalence holds across multiple sites or reflects a single outbreak snapshot. But the directional finding is robust: plague's emergence as a human killer predates agriculture and dense settlement, which reframes our understanding of pathogen evolution more broadly.

### Major Errors Found in Al Gore-founded Climate TRACE Database

> **Summary.** An independent audit found Climate TRACE's emissions database undercounts CO₂ from urban vehicles by an average of 70% across 260 U.S. cities, with some gaps exceeding 90%. The discrepancy raises serious questions about the database's use in municipal climate planning and corporate carbon accounting.

This is practice-changing in the negative: a widely cited emissions tool appears systematically wrong in ways that matter for policy. Climate TRACE *(AI-powered emissions-tracking consortium co-founded by Al Gore, using satellite data and machine learning to estimate global greenhouse gas emissions)* has been embraced by cities, investors, and corporations as a standardized emissions source. Northern Arizona University's audit found the vehicle emissions gap by comparing TRACE estimates against actual fuel sales and traffic data — a ground-truth check that machine learning models often fail to undergo.

The framing here is worth watching. "Raising red flags" understates what looks like a structural problem: if ML models trained on proxy data miss urban combustion by this magnitude, other sectors may be similarly off. What's strong is the methodological clarity — fuel sales are hard to fake, and the 260-city sample isn't cherry-picked. What's unknown is whether TRACE's errors are consistent (systematically low everywhere, which permits relative comparison) or variable by city type (which would corrupt rankings and prioritization). The consortium's response will matter; if they don't commit to ground-truth validation protocols, users should treat this as directional at best.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260618041519.htm))
