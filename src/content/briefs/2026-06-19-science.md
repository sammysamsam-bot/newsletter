### The genome's 3D structure defies AI's flat-file assumptions

> **Summary.** Biologists have long treated DNA as a linear blueprint or algorithm, but new work emphasizes its physical entanglement in space. This *chromatin architecture* — how DNA loops, folds, and sticks to itself in the nucleus — may be why AI models trained on sequence data alone keep missing regulatory patterns. The tension is methodological: machine learning wants tidy inputs, while the genome operates as a dynamic, sticky, three-dimensional tangle.

Quanta's piece argues that the "genome as code" metaphor has led AI systems astray, and that progress in predicting gene expression from sequence requires modeling physical proximity, not just base-pair order. The evidence here is conceptual rather than experimental — the article builds on recent structural biology and connects it to ongoing failures in *cis*-regulatory prediction. What's strong: the framing accurately captures why DeepMind's AlphaFold and similar tools excel at protein structure but struggle with regulatory logic. What's thin: no specific AI model is named as failing for this reason, and the "may confound" hedging is doing real work. The piece reads as a methodological provocation, not a result.

The broader point lands anyway. Biology has a recurring problem of exporting its metaphors to engineering before they're stable — "code," "blueprint," "circuit" — and this is a corrective worth sitting with. Whether it shifts practice depends on whether foundation-model labs building DNA LLMs *(large language models trained on genomic sequence)* start incorporating Hi-C *(high-throughput chromosome conformation capture, a technique to map 3D genome structure)* data as native inputs rather than post-hoc features.

([Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-the-human-genomes-tangled-physicality-may-confound-ai-20260618/))

### Alzheimer's: amyloid-tau crosstalk may be the real trigger

> **Summary.** A new study reframes Alzheimer's pathology: amyloid beta plaques may not directly cause neuron death but instead disrupt tau, the protein that normally stabilizes neuronal structure. This interference could be the initiating event that cascades into the recognizable brain changes of the disease. If the mechanism holds, it would explain why anti-amyloid drugs clear plaques without always slowing cognitive decline.

The ScienceDaily report frames this as a "hidden trigger" discovery, and the mechanism is genuinely interesting — amyloid as tau-disruptor rather than amyloid as toxin. Evidence quality is medium: the summary doesn't specify whether this is human tissue work, mouse models, or in vitro, and "scientists may have found" is doing heavy lifting. The strongest implication is therapeutic: if tau disruption is the bottleneck, combination therapies or tau-stabilizing approaches become more urgent. The thin part is timing — we've been through several "real cause of Alzheimer's" reframings, and this needs replication across independent cohorts before it changes trial design.

Still, the discrepancy between plaque clearance and clinical benefit in drugs like lecanemab and donanemab has been a real puzzle. This offers a coherent explanation for that gap, which is more than most amyloid alternatives have managed.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260617032209.htm))

### Southern California faults hit 1,000-year stress peak; "earthquake gate" identified

> **Summary.** Researchers find that California's Cajon Pass — where the San Andreas and San Jacinto faults intersect — is more stressed than at any point in the last millennium. This junction appears to function as an *earthquake gate*, a chokepoint that determines whether ruptures propagate across both fault systems or remain contained. Current conditions resemble those preceding historically large quakes.

The evidence here mixes paleoseismology *(study of ancient earthquakes via geological records)* with GPS-derived strain rates, and the 1,000-year claim depends on trenching studies and dating accuracy that's necessarily uncertain. What's strong: the "gate" concept is well-established in fault mechanics, and the San Andreas-San Jacinto intersection has long been recognized as a likely multi-fault rupture point. What's thin: "resemble conditions that preceded" is pattern-matching across sparse historical samples, and the summary doesn't specify whether this is published, in review, or presented at a meeting.

The practical implication is real enough — emergency planners already weight this junction highly, and the framing doesn't change response protocols so much as reinforce them. The scientific value is in probabilistic rupture forecasting: if we can identify which gates are likely to open, we move from single-fault to multi-fault hazard models, which matters enormously for building codes and insurance. But the "highest stress in 1,000 years" headline risks overprecision on inherently noisy data.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260617032155.htm))
