### GLP-1 drugs linked to lower addiction and overdose risk across six substance classes

> **Summary.** A retrospective study of 600,000+ U.S. veterans found that *semaglutide* *(Ozempic/Wegovy, GLP-1 receptor agonist)* and similar drugs were associated with reduced incidence of substance use disorders involving alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, cocaine, opioids, and other drugs. Those already addicted showed fewer overdoses, hospitalizations, and drug-related deaths. The effect is correlational, not causal.

The signal here is genuinely striking: six distinct pharmacological classes, all moving in the same protective direction. That's the kind of pattern that makes you pay attention, even in a large observational study where confounding is inevitable. The veterans population carries elevated baseline risk for both metabolic disease and substance use, so if GLP-1s are somehow blurring reward pathways generally—not just food reward—this could reshape how we think about their mechanism. What's thin is the *how*. These are health records, not randomized trials; people prescribed GLP-1s may differ in unmeasured ways from controls, and the effect sizes aren't reported in the summary. The "food noise" piece from Nautilus *(intrusive thoughts about food, colloquial term for preoccupation)* makes a useful companion read here—there's growing speculation that GLP-1s act on broader impulse and reward circuitry, not just gastric emptying.

Still, the overdose and mortality endpoints are hard endpoints, and the sheer scale of the cohort lends weight. If this replicates in trial data, we'd be looking at a repurposing story as significant as the original diabetes-to-obesity pivot. For now, it's the strongest population-level hint yet that these drugs do something neurologically we don't fully understand.

### "Magic" quantum measure tied to gravity in holographic space-time

> **Summary.** Physicists report that *magic* *(a measure of quantum computational complexity, quantifying how far a quantum state is from stabilizer states)* in holographic theories may explain why space-time is pliable enough to curve into gravity. The work connects two previously separate programs: entanglement as the fabric of space-time, and the computational resources needed to generate quantum states.

This is methodology-shifting in ambition if not yet in confirmed result. The holographic principle *(conjecture that a gravitational volume is encoded on its lower-dimensional boundary, as in AdS/CFT)* has already absorbed entanglement entropy as the thing that "knits together" space-time geometry; what this group argues is that entanglement alone produces rigid, non-dynamical space-time, and you need the extra ingredient of magic to get the curvature that obeys Einstein's equations. The evidence quality is high *within* the theoretical framework—they've derived a precise mathematical relationship in a specific toy model—but the usual caveats about holography apply. This is not yet a prediction we can test against observed gravity.

Where it earns its place is the conceptual move: treating gravity not as geometry or field, but as a *resource constraint* on quantum simulation. That's a framing shift that could propagate. The article doesn't overclaim; it notes the derivation holds for a restricted class of states and that "magic" remains computationally expensive to measure even in principle. We don't know if nature actually uses this particular mechanism. What we know is that the math closes in a way it didn't before.

### New diabetes pill burns fat through muscle metabolism, preserving lean mass

> **Summary.** An experimental oral drug activates metabolism in skeletal muscle rather than suppressing appetite, lowering blood sugar and increasing fat burning while preserving muscle mass in early clinical data. It targets a distinct pathway from *semaglutide* *(GLP-1 receptor agonist)* and related drugs. Safety and tolerability look acceptable in the early cohort.

The "without the downsides of Ozempic" framing in the headline does more work than the data supports so far—we don't have phase 3 outcomes, we don't have long-term safety, and "preserving muscle mass" is a biomarker, not a functional endpoint. But the mechanism is genuinely different: where GLP-1s work centrally on appetite and peripherally on insulin secretion, this compound appears to increase *basal energy expenditure* in muscle tissue specifically. That's the holy grail of obesity pharmacology, because current weight-loss drugs lose 20-40% of lost mass as lean tissue, which is particularly dangerous in older patients.

The evidence quality is early-phase only: the summary mentions "early clinical results" without specifying phase, sample size, duration, or whether the data was placebo-controlled. That's thin. What makes it worth including is the strategic implication. The GLP-1 class has set a high bar for efficacy, but its side-effect profile (nausea, muscle loss, potential psychiatric effects, unknown long-term) leaves room for a parallel drug class with a cleaner tradeoff. If muscle-centric metabolism activation works at scale, it could complement or compete. We'd need to see peer-reviewed data to move this from "worth tracking" to "practice-changing."
