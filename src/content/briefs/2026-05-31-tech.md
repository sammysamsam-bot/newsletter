### OMB proposes sweeping grant-cancellation authority over federal science funding

> **Summary.** The White House Office of Management and Budget *(OMB, presidential budget office)* circulated draft rules that would let political appointees cancel any federal grant at any time, make peer review optional, and screen applications for "forbidden topics." The move builds on earlier Trump-administration efforts to reshape NIH and NSF grant-making. Science groups say the language would end the independence of merit review that has underpinned U.S. research since WWII.

The article lays out the specifics with unusual clarity: grants could be terminated without cause, appeals would go through political staff, and agencies would no longer have to use independent experts to evaluate scientific proposals. The evidence here is strong because Ars Technica reproduces the actual OMB memo language and contrasts it with existing federal regulations. The historical framing—that this dismantles a post-WWII consensus on merit-based science funding—is asserted more than demonstrated; the piece gestures at Vannevar Bush without really establishing that the proposed system is historically unprecedented rather than just worse. What's genuinely thin is the "forbidden topics" list: Ars Technica says the memo references them but doesn't quote them, so we're relying on the publication's characterization of a document we can't see.

Where the piece earns its keep is in showing this isn't a one-off. It ties the OMB draft to the earlier NIH indirect-cost cap, the firings of probationary federal scientists, and the shuttering of infectious-disease centers now hindering Ebola response. That clustering makes the case that there's a systematic pattern, not just bureaucratic friction. The counterargument—that OMB is floating trial balloons it knows courts will block—goes unexamined.

### SoftBank pledges €75B for French data-center buildout; OpenRouter closes $113M Series B

> **Summary.** SoftBank committed up to €75 billion for up to 5 gigawatts of data-center capacity in France, its largest European infrastructure bet yet. Days earlier, OpenRouter *(unified API for routing requests across AI models)* raised $113 million in Series B funding. The two deals illustrate diverging bets in AI infrastructure: SoftBank on raw power generation and real estate, OpenRouter on model-agnostic serving layers.

SoftBank's French pledge is consequential because 5GW represents roughly double France's current data-center footprint; this is nation-scale industrial policy dressed as private investment. The TechCrunch reporting is thin on whether SoftBank has secured grid interconnection agreements or just MOUs with regional governments—that distinction determines whether this is a 2028 reality or a 2035 aspiration. We don't know the offtake structure: is SoftBank building speculatively, or do hyperscalers *(cloud providers at massive scale: Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta)* already have letters of intent?

OpenRouter's raise is more immediately legible. The company has become the default abstraction layer for developers who don't want to be locked into any single model provider; its API now routes to dozens of open-weights and closed models. The funding-round announcement claims 500,000 developers, which we can't verify but which aligns with its ubiquity in AI-engineering discourse. What's unspoken in the release: OpenRouter's margin structure depends on provider pricing that is itself unstable. If Anthropic or OpenAI change their token economics, OpenRouter's take rate gets squeezed unless it passes costs through—which would test developer loyalty.

### Ghost Angels fund formalizes Snap alumni network for AI-native social bets

> **Summary.** Ghost Angels *(venture fund launched by ~20 former Snap employees, including ex-global partnerships head Max Rivera and founding product member Will Wu)* has unveiled its formal structure, backing pre-seed AI startups building creative tools and niche community platforms. The fund has already invested in at least five companies and targets at least fifteen more, betting that the ad-heavy social model is losing appeal.

This is a cluster item by necessity—there's no single dramatic event, just the formalization of an angel network that was already operating. TechCrunch's reporting leans heavily on participant self-description ("diverse deal-sourcing," "founder support") without independent verification of returns or portfolio quality. What's actually interesting is the thesis: these are insiders from a company that never figured out sustainable monetization at scale, now betting that AI can unlock what Snap couldn't. The tension goes unexamined. If the ad model failed for Snap because of platform dependency on Apple and Google, do AI-native tools escape that trap, or just recreate it?

The fund's existing investments aren't named, so we can't evaluate whether it's finding genuinely new distribution models or just recycling familiar social-media mechanics with LLM *(large language model)* wrappers. The operators' claim that "early-career talent" mixed with senior leadership improves sourcing is plausible but untested at venture scale.
