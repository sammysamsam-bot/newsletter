### Nvidia commits $150B/year to Taiwan AI build-out, defying US reshoring push

> **Summary.** Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said Taiwan will be the "epicenter" of AI infrastructure, pledging roughly $150 billion annually in investment—more than double the company's prior capital spending guidance. The framing is a direct rebuke of Trump administration industrial policy aimed at making the US an AI manufacturing hub. *(TSMC)*, Nvidia's sole foundry for advanced AI chips, is headquartered in Taiwan.

The announcement lands as Washington's CHIPS Act subsidies and tariff threats have failed to meaningfully shift leading-edge semiconductor fabrication to US soil. Intel's foundry struggles and TSMC's Arizona delays underline the gap between policy ambition and execution. Huang's rhetoric is unusually pointed for a CEO navigating US-China-Taiwan crosswinds—he explicitly contrasted Taiwan's ecosystem depth with American alternatives. The $150B figure warrants scrutiny: Nvidia's entire 2024 revenue was ~$130B, so this implies partner-funded capex (TSMC, Foxconn, suppliers) rolled into a headline number. What's strong is the clarity of Nvidia's bet on status quo geopolitics; what's thin is whether this spending is net-new or rebranded existing supply chain commitments.

For the US reshoring narrative, this is a material setback dressed as foreign investment. The policy implication is sharper than the financial one—Huang is effectively lobbying for continued Taiwan Strait stability as a US strategic interest, not despite American jobs rhetoric but in direct competition with it. ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/nvidia-ceo-wants-taiwan-to-be-center-of-ai-revolution-not-us/))

### SpaceX wins Space Force "sensor-to-shooter" contract for military satellite targeting mesh

> **Summary.** The US Space Force confirmed SpaceX will build a network linking surveillance satellites directly to weapon systems, cutting out ground-based processing delays. The "Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture" *(PWSA, a constellation of small satellites in low-Earth orbit)* aims for real-time target handoff. A program manager stated: "We aren't trading speed for scale; we are demanding both."

This is autonomy infrastructure at scale—machine-speed targeting decisions with minimal human in the loop, which is why it matters more than another defense contract headline. The article's evidence is solid on program structure and quoting, thin on technical architecture (how much edge AI versus ground processing, what's actually automated versus assisted). SpaceX's Starshield *(military-grade Starlink variant with encrypted, jam-resistant links)* is the presumed backbone, though the piece doesn't confirm this. The "sensor-to-shooter" framing has migrated from Air Force doctrine to procurement reality faster than most expected; Space Force's willingness to bet on a commercial provider with launch integration is the notable shift. Skepticism owed on timeline—constellation deployments this complex historically slip, and "real-time" in procurement briefings rarely survives first contact with contested spectrum. ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/05/us-space-force-confirms-spacex-will-build-sensor-to-shooter-targeting-network/))

### Browser-based SSD fingerprinting opens new surveillance channel

> **Summary.** Researchers demonstrated that JavaScript can measure solid-state drive *(SSD)* activity patterns through timing analysis, creating a persistent tracking vector even with cookies blocked and fingerprinting defenses active. The attack exploits how browsers handle storage access latency, distinguishing users by their drive's unique performance characteristics.

The security story here is genuine and substantively reported. The Ars piece walks through methodology responsibly: the attack requires specific conditions (cache partitioning bypasses, repeated measurements), but the underlying vector—using hardware-level timing side channels for deanonymization—is well-established in academic literature and now shown practical in commodity browsers. What's strong is the proof-of-concept validation across drive types and browsers; what's thin is real-world exploit prevalence (none claimed) and mitigations (Chrome and Firefox teams are aware, no patches shipped). The framing as "websites have a new way to spy" slightly overstates immediacy—this is research-stage, not in-the-wild. Still, the trend it represents matters: as browser vendors patch higher-level tracking, surveillance interest migrates to hardware-timing channels that are structurally harder to eliminate without performance cost. Worth watching whether this enters the *W3C* privacy threat model formally. ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/05/websites-have-a-new-way-to-spy-on-visitors-analyzing-their-ssd-activity/))
