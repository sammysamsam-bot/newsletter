### US Space Force taps SpaceX to build sensor-to-shooter targeting network

> **Summary.** The U.S. Space Force *(newest military branch, established 2019 to consolidate satellite and space-based defense operations)* has confirmed SpaceX will build a military network linking orbital sensors directly to weapons systems. The "sensor-to-shooter" architecture aims to compress the kill chain from detection to engagement, with a program manager stating they're demanding "speed and scale" simultaneously.

This is a concrete autonomy primitive with real procurement dollars behind it. The Space Force release frames the contract as part of a broader shift toward "proliferated architectures" — essentially replacing a few exquisite satellites with many cheaper, networked nodes. What makes this worth tracking is the vendor choice: SpaceX's Starshield *(military-grade Starlink derivative)* has been positioned for this, but the announcement confirms it's moving from pitch deck to operational infrastructure. The evidence quality is decent — official program office statements, named contracting officer — though we don't have dollar figures or delivery timelines yet. The rhetoric about "not trading speed for scale" reads as slightly defensive; past Pentagon space programs have famously failed by chasing both simultaneously.

The military logic here is straightforward but consequential. Current targeting pipelines can take minutes to hours as data bounces between classified and unclassified networks, terrestrial stations, and regional command centers. A space-based mesh with direct weapon links collapses that. What's thin is how much redundancy exists when nodes are inevitably contested. China's demonstrated anti-satellite capabilities make "proliferated" sound resilient, but the actual network topology and node count remain classified. We'll know this is real when exercises show measured latency figures, not just slide decks.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/05/us-space-force-confirms-spacex-will-build-sensor-to-shooter-targeting-network/))

### Nvidia commits $150B/year to Taiwan AI infrastructure

> **Summary.** Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang announced plans to invest $150 billion annually to make Taiwan an AI "epicenter," a move that undercuts Trump administration rhetoric about US-based semiconductor manufacturing. The spending prioritizes advanced packaging and foundry capacity in Taiwan over Arizona fabs that have struggled with delays and cost overruns.

This is an infra shift with teeth — the dollar figure is large even by Nvidia's standards, and the geopolitical framing is deliberate. Huang's timing matters: the announcement comes as TSMC's Arizona investments face continued skepticism and Trump's tariff threats rattle supply chains. The evidence is strong on the commitment itself — direct quotes, press conference, named dollar figure — but we should note what's unsaid. Nvidia isn't abandoning US presence; this looks like a hedging strategy where Taiwan gets the cutting-edge volume while Arizona handles politically sensitive but smaller-scale production. The "backfires" in the headline does more work than Huang's actual words, which were careful to praise both locations.

The real story is supply chain realism colliding with industrial policy theater. Advanced packaging *(chip-stacking and interconnection technology critical for AI accelerators)* remains concentrated in Taiwan for good reason: the ecosystem of chemical suppliers, equipment technicians, and tacit process knowledge doesn't relocate on political timelines. Nvidia's bet is that Washington's pressure will soften before Taiwan's technical advantages erode. That's a political take worth owning, not a fact — but it's informed by Arizona's repeated delays and the basic physics of where CoWoS *(TSMC's chip-on-wafer-on-substrate technology, essential for H100/B200 production)* capacity actually exists today.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/nvidia-ceo-wants-taiwan-to-be-center-of-ai-revolution-not-us/))

### Websites can fingerprint visitors via SSD activity timing

> **Summary.** Researchers have demonstrated that browser JavaScript can measure telltale timing patterns from a visitor's solid-state drive *(SSD)* *(non-volatile flash storage replacing mechanical hard drives)* to create a persistent tracking fingerprint. The attack exploits how SSDs handle read/write operations differently based on wear levels and data layout, leaking identifiable timing signatures through seemingly innocuous browser storage APIs.

This is a genuine security advance with clearly explained stakes. The research team showed that SSD-specific characteristics — not just browser cookies or canvas fingerprints — create cross-session identifiers that resist standard privacy tools. The evidence quality is strong: the Ars piece links the original paper, explains the JavaScript measurement mechanism, and notes tested browsers. What's particularly useful is how they define the attack scope: this is local privilege in the sense that any site you visit can attempt it, not remote code execution, but the tracking persistence is the harm.

Two things warrant honest doubt. First, the practical exploitability against privacy-conscious users running Firefox with strict storage limits isn't fully tested — the paper focuses on default configurations. Second, mitigation is currently thin: browser vendors haven't shipped defenses, and the SSD industry's standardization bodies weren't designed for privacy threat models. The "spy" framing in the headline isn't wrong, but it slightly overstates current deployment; we have proof-of-concept, not mass adoption. Still, this belongs in the section because it's a foundational shift in what's considered fingerprintable surface area. Previously we assumed storage was opaque to websites; this shows the physical medium leaks through timing side channels.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/05/websites-have-a-new-way-to-spy-on-visitors-analyzing-their-ssd-activity/))
