### Nvidia bets $150B annual investment on Taiwan as US AI hub plans stall

> **Summary.** Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang announced plans to invest $150 billion per year in Taiwan to establish an AI "epicenter," directly contradicting Trump administration efforts to shift advanced semiconductor manufacturing to the US. The commitment leverages Taiwan's existing TSMC ecosystem and supply chain density. *(TSMC: Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, the world's largest contract chipmaker)*

The framing here is deliberate political theater as much as industrial strategy. Huang's statement that Taiwan "should be the center" rather than the US is a rare public counterweight from a CEO who typically navigates Washington carefully. The $150B figure is striking — roughly triple TSMC's annual capex — but the article leaves ambiguous whether this represents new Nvidia spending, supply-chain commitments, or a looser ecosystem valuation. What's solid: Nvidia's Blackwell chips are already in volume production at TSMC, and the company has no plausible near-term alternative for leading-edge packaging. The "backfires" framing of Trump's US hub plan is premature; the CHIPS Act subsidies are still flowing, and Intel's foundry efforts, however troubled, haven't collapsed. The stronger read is that Huang is protecting his supply chain by flattering a Taiwanese government increasingly nervous about US reliability.

The geopolitical risk is the real story. Concentrating $150B in annual investment on an island China claims is unsustainable strategically, which both Washington and Beijing know. Nvidia's bet assumes either that cross-strait stability holds through the decade, or that no alternative exists fast enough to matter. Neither assumption is obviously wrong, but neither is obviously safe. The article doesn't press Huang on contingency planning — a gap worth noting.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/nvidia-ceo-wants-taiwan-to-be-center-of-ai-revolution-not-us/))

### New browser fingerprinting technique exploits SSD activity patterns

> **Summary.** Researchers demonstrated that websites can fingerprint visitors by analyzing solid-state drive *(SSD)* activity timing through JavaScript, using the browser's Battery Status API and performance.now() to detect distinctive read/write patterns. The attack requires no special permissions and works across browsers. The finding extends a known vulnerability class rather than introducing a fundamentally new attack surface.

The Ars Technica coverage is technically careful where it could have been alarmist. The attack isn't reading your files — it's measuring how your specific SSD responds to background system tasks, creating a persistent identifier that survives cookie clearing. The researchers' proof-of-concept distinguishes individual machines with what they claim is high reliability, though the article doesn't specify false-positive rates or test population size, which we'd want before assessing practical risk. What's genuinely notable: this joins a growing pile of browser fingerprinting techniques that bypass user-consent frameworks entirely. The "no permissions required" aspect is the regulatory hook — it implicates GDPR and state privacy laws that assume users can meaningfully consent to tracking.

The defense situation is bleak. Browser vendors have deprecated Battery Status API in part for similar abuse, but performance timing APIs are too deeply embedded in web compatibility to restrict. The article's closing note that "standard security advice doesn't cover this" is accurate and underplayed. We're in a position where individual browser hardening is insufficient and regulatory frameworks are structurally mismatched to implementation-level attacks. Not a breach-your-bank-account threat today, but a steady erosion of anonymity expectations that matters for journalists, activists, and increasingly ordinary users in adversarial legal environments.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/05/websites-have-a-new-way-to-spy-on-visitos-analyzing-their-ssd-activity/))

### Volvo secures exemption from US connected-car ban on Chinese tech

> **Summary.** The Commerce Department approved Volvo's request to bypass Biden-era rules prohibiting Chinese-connected vehicle hardware and software in model year 2027 vehicles. The exemption covers Volvo's existing supply agreements and connected-car systems, which rely on Chinese components and software infrastructure. The decision represents a concrete regulatory carve-out rather than a policy reversal.

The action reporting here is straightforward, but the implications deserve attention. The connected-car ban was framed as a national-security measure — Chinese-made hardware and software in vehicles were deemed potential surveillance or sabotage vectors. Granting Volvo an exemption on supply-chain practicality grounds undermines that framing without officially weakening it. Either the security risk was overstated, or Commerce is accepting elevated risk for a single manufacturer's commercial convenience. The article doesn't clarify the exemption's duration, scope, or whether other automakers have similar requests pending, all of which would tell us if this is a one-off or a template.

The Trump administration's posture is mixed: maintaining the Biden rule nominally while creating exceptions that soften its bite. This matches patterns in tariff and tech-export policy, but the security specificity of connected vehicles makes the tension sharper. Volvo's Chinese ownership by Geely is the unexamined angle — the exemption benefits a Chinese-parented company more than, say, Ford would benefit from similar relief. The article notes Geely ownership but doesn't press whether it influenced or complicated the decision. That's a gap worth flagging; the intersection of ownership structure and security exemption is where this story likely develops.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/cars/2026/05/volvo-gets-us-government-approval-to-bypass-chinese-connected-car-ban/))
