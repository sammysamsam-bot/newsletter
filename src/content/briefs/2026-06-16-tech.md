### UK moves to ban social media for under-16s, with overnight curfews in play

> **Summary.** The UK government will introduce legislation barring children under 16 from social media platforms and is considering mandatory overnight curfews for teen users. Enforcement mechanisms remain vague — VPN circumvention is already being flagged by critics as a practical gap. The policy follows Australia's similar 2025 ban and represents a significant regulatory experiment in age-gating the open internet.

This is consequential regulation that will test platform architecture and legal frameworks alike. The Ars Technica piece notes the familiar pattern: policymakers assume bans redirect behavior, while teenagers and technologists immediately identify circumvention paths. The framing does honest work here — it doesn't treat the VPN objection as a refutation, but as a design constraint the policy hasn't addressed. What's thin is the actual implementation detail: we don't know whether this becomes a duty-of-care model (platforms liable for underage access) or a parental-governance model, and that distinction determines whether this reshapes product design or merely generates compliance theater. The overnight curfew element is genuinely novel — time-based gating at the OS or ISP level — and the article rightly flags it as technically plausible but politically charged.

Worth watching how Meta, TikTok, and Snap respond architecturally. If the UK extracts meaningful platform changes, the EU and US will notice; if it devolves into checkbox age-verification, it joins the pile of unenforced digital regulation. The clustering with Australia, and the broader list of countries moving similarly, suggests this is becoming a definitional fight over "internet adulthood" — 16 vs. 13, parental consent vs. blanket prohibition — that will outlast any single government's tenure.

### AMD quietly stripped memory encryption from consumer CPUs, and the backlash is revealing

> **Summary.** AMD removed Transparent Secure Memory Encryption *(TSME — hardware-level RAM encryption previously standard on Ryzen processors)* from consumer chips without announcement, only confirming the change after user investigation. The feature persists in server-grade Epyc CPUs. AMD frames this as product segmentation; critics read it as degrading baseline security for mainstream users without notice.

The Ars Technica report is methodically reported — it tracks forum discovery, AMD's eventual confirmation, and the company's oblique justification about "aligning features with customer needs." The evidence quality is high on the factual sequence (TSME gone, AMD silent, then grudgingly responsive) but weaker on motivation. AMD's claim that consumers weren't using TSME is plausible but unverified; the counter-claim that this prepares a future upsell to "Pro" tiers is speculative but grounded in established semiconductor pricing strategy. What's genuinely interesting is the governance angle: hardware feature removal via unannounced microcode or stepping changes is effectively invisible to most purchasers, and there's no regulatory requirement for disclosure. The security community's anger is less about TSME's practical value — it's a niche protection against physical memory attacks — than about the transparency failure. If vendors can subtract security features silently, trust in the supply chain erodes for everyone, including enterprise buyers who do depend on these guarantees.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/06/users-cry-foul-after-amd-stripped-memory-crypto-from-its-consumer-cpus/))

### Nvidia tests debt-market appetite with $25B bond sale amid AI infrastructure buildout

> **Summary.** Nvidia is raising over $25 billion in its first corporate bond issuance since 2021, joining a crowded field of tech borrowers funding data center expansion. The offering tests whether investors will absorb additional AI-sector exposure after a period of heavy capital expenditure across hyperscalers. Proceeds likely target general corporate purposes including the accelerated buildout of GPU clusters and networking infrastructure.

This sits at the intersection of several live stories: the AI infrastructure arms race, rising corporate leverage, and the question of whether demand for AI compute keeps pace with supply buildout. The Ars Technica piece is appropriately restrained — it notes the "deluge of borrowing" context without predicting a bubble. What's strong is the comparative framing: Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta have all issued heavily in recent quarters, so Nvidia's entry signals that even the dominant silicon vendor sees debt financing as cheaper or more strategic than equity dilution at current valuations. What's thin is the use-of-proceeds specificity — Nvidia's filing language will be generic, and the article doesn't speculate beyond "general corporate purposes." The consequential read is balance-sheet risk transfer: Nvidia is exceptionally cash-generative now, but locking in long-duration debt before potential rate cuts or demand softening is classic treasury strategy. Whether this is smart financial engineering or euphemism for "we need more capital than free cash flow provides" depends on Q2 earnings and capex guidance.
