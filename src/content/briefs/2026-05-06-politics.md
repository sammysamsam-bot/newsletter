### Trump’s Vengeance Tour Clears Five Indiana Republicans in State Senate Primaries

#### The revenge

- Trump endorsed eight challengers to GOP state senators who had voted against his push to redraw Indiana’s congressional map — an extraordinary presidential incursion into a state-level matter.
- At least five incumbents lost, including Tax and Fiscal Policy Chair Travis Holdman, who told a reporter afterward: “Welcome to D.C. politics in Indiana because this means that’s what’s coming.”

#### The map that started it

- The Indiana map already heavily favors Republicans; Trump’s allies argued it didn’t go far enough to lock in safe seats for the midterms.
- The defeated legislators viewed the intervention as a breach of state prerogative and an effort to punish any deviation from the White House line.

#### Context

Trump’s ability to terminate incumbents in a low-turnout primary is real, and it will reshape the Indiana Senate as a loyalty-first body. What these primaries don’t answer is whether the new loyalists are more or less electable in general elections — or whether the party just traded bench depth for purity in a state where the fight for the House majority runs through districts a thousand miles away. The revenge worked. The general-election price tag hasn’t been priced yet.

### The Food-Stamp Work-Requirement Debate Is Fighting Over the Wrong Yardstick

#### What the research says

- A new study concludes that work requirements attached to SNAP benefits did not increase employment among recipients — undermining the core rationale for tying food aid to a job search.
- Researchers looked at the data after previous state-level experiments and found no meaningful boost in workforce participation.

#### What the administration claims

- Since tightening work rules via the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the sprawling budget bill passed this year, states have cut food-stamp rolls by 3.5 million people.
- The White House and allied editorial pages frame those reductions as cutting government bloat and driving Americans “off the couch and back into the labor force.”

#### The yardstick problem

- Shrinking enrollment is not the same as rising employment. People can be removed for paperwork errors, reporting hurdles, or hitting time limits without ever finding a job.
- The study measures behavior change; the administration is measuring caseload shrinkage. Neither tells us whether those 3.5 million people are working, or simply unassisted.

#### Context

A proper employment-tracking survey that follows households after their benefits end would settle this. No one has published one yet. Until a longitudinal study exists, each side will pick the metric that fits its story — the researchers counting jobs that didn’t materialize, the administration counting cases that closed. The gap between those numbers is where the policy’s real-world cost lands.

### The White House Puts a $529 Billion Price Tag on Its Drug Deals, Right Before the Midterms

#### The number

- White House economists estimate that Trump’s deals with pharmaceutical companies to bring U.S. prescription-drug prices in line with what other countries pay would save the economy $529 billion over a decade.
- The analysis, obtained by the Associated Press, is the first government-wide projection of the policy’s macroeconomic impact.

#### The doubts

- Democratic lawmakers have called the claimed savings into question, noting that previous estimates of the same framework were far smaller and that enforcement mechanisms remain unclear.
- The figure comes from an internal White House document, not from the Congressional Budget Office or another independent scoring body, and the full methodology hasn’t been released.

#### Context

Drug-pricing reform is one of the administration’s strongest electoral pitches, and putting a ten-figure number behind it is a clear midterm move. But the number’s credibility depends entirely on fine print: which drugs are covered, how “international price” is defined, and what happens when a company refuses to play ball. Without an independent score, this reads less like a forecast and more like a negotiating position dressed as a projection. A CBO response or an industry counter-estimate is the next signal to watch.
