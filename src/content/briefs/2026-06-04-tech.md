### Google ordered to surface clearer sources in AI Overviews, let UK publishers opt out

> **Summary.** The UK's Competition and Markets Authority *(CMA, antitrust regulator)* has ordered Google to make AI Overviews cite sources more prominently and give UK news publishers a choice to appear in them. The ruling follows Google's own research claiming users didn't want "lots of sources" — a framing the CMA rejected. Publishers can now opt out of AI Overviews while remaining in standard search.

Google's defense was essentially "users prefer the clean look," which the CMA treated as the self-serving argument it is. The stronger evidence here is behavioral: when Google tested more explicit sourcing, click-through rates to publishers didn't collapse, which undermines the "users hate links" narrative. What's thin is the enforcement mechanism — the CMA can fine Google up to 10% of global revenue, but actual CMA follow-through on tech orders has been patchy. The real test is whether publishers *en masse* opt out and whether that degrades Overview quality enough that Google voluntarily improves the product. The ruling also ducks the harder question: AI Overviews still ingest publisher content for training whether the publisher opts out of display or not. This is a display-layer fix, not a copyright-layer one.

The publisher angle matters beyond the UK. Google's AI search product is under similar pressure in Australia, Canada, and from EU copyright directive implementation. A UK precedent makes coordinated publisher bargaining more viable. But note what this isn't: it's not a ruling on whether generative AI search itself violates copyright, just on fair dealing in the presentation layer. The deeper litigation — NYT v. OpenAI, the ongoing Getty cases — remains unresolved.

### Dashlane's opaque breach notice: 20 million encrypted vaults stolen, details scarce

> **Summary.** Password manager Dashlane disclosed that threat actors stole roughly 20 million encrypted vaults from AWS S3 buckets *(cloud object storage)*, but the security advisory omitted critical context: whether vault encryption keys were compromised, how attackers accessed the buckets, and whether any vaults used weaker legacy encryption. Dashlane has since gone silent, declining press inquiries.

The advisory is a case study in disclosure theater. It states the vaults are "encrypted with user master passwords" but doesn't clarify whether all 20 million used Argon2id *(modern password-hashing function)* or whether older accounts still used PBKDF2-SHA1 *(weaker predecessor)*, which Dashlane was migrating away from. The distinction matters: Argon2id vaults remain practically uncrackable; PBKDF2-SHA1 vaults with weak master passwords are vulnerable to offline brute-force. The article's frustration is warranted — Dashlane is asking users to assess their own risk without giving them the variables to do so. The "no evidence of access" formulation is also doing heavy lifting; absence of logs is not evidence of absence when attackers had S3 access.

What we can say: encrypted vaults are not plaintext passwords, but they're not nothing. The threat model shifts from "credentials exposed immediately" to "attackers can attempt to crack vaults at leisure, prioritizing targets by metadata." If you reused a weak master password, rotated it now. The stronger question is whether Dashlane's architecture — encrypting vaults server-side before S3 storage — was the right call versus local-only encryption like Bitwarden's model. This incident feeds that debate without resolving it.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/06/dashlane-issues-opaque-advisory-warning-20-encrypted-vaults-were-stolen/))

### Robotaxis don't cut traffic — they're empty for half their miles

> **Summary.** A new study found Waymo's robotaxis in San Francisco and Phoenix drive empty almost 50% of their miles — comparable to Uber and Lyft deadheading *(driving without passengers to reposition or pick up)*. The traffic-reduction promise of autonomous vehicles assumes efficient fleet utilization; the data suggests we're not close.

The study's methodology is its strength: GPS-inferred occupancy matched against actual Waymo trip data, cross-referenced with California DMV and Phoenix reporting. The near-50% deadhead rate is damning because it undermines the core efficiency argument for autonomous taxi fleets. Human-driven ridehail already wastes road capacity on repositioning; robotaxis were supposed to optimize this away through better dispatch, demand prediction, and centralized fleet management. Instead they're matching or slightly exceeding human-driver inefficiency. The framing in some coverage — "autonomous vehicles were supposed to cut traffic" — slightly overstates the study's scope; this is robotaxi fleet operations, not all AV use cases. Private autonomous vehicles might still reduce parking demand, and highway platooning could improve throughput.

What's unexplored: whether Waymo's deadheading is structural (spread-out demand in suburban Phoenix) or operational (deliberately keeping vehicles moving to reduce passenger wait times). The company has incentives to optimize for customer pickup speed, not system-wide road efficiency. A public policy angle the article touches lightly: if robotaxis get dedicated lanes or curb priority, their inefficiency gets subsidized by everyone else's congestion. The traffic argument for AV privileges just got weaker.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/cars/2026/06/robotaxis-dont-cut-traffic-any-more-than-ride-hailing-study-finds/))
