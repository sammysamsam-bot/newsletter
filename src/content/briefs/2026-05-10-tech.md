### Court rules Trump's 10% tariff is just as illegal as the tariff it replaced

> **Summary.** The U.S. Court of International Trade struck down the 10% blanket tariff Trump imposed after an earlier ruling invalidated his "reciprocal" tariffs, finding both exceed presidential trade authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act *(IEEPA, a 1977 law giving presidents limited emergency economic powers)*. The administration immediately appealed and Trump vowed to impose tariffs "a different way" if necessary.

The ruling is consequential because it closes the loophole Trump tried after his first tariff structure failed — he simply swapped in a flat 10% rate and kept going. The court wasn't fooled: the statutory overreach is the same, regardless of the number attached. For tech specifically, the Ars piece notes industry anxiety about what "a different way" means — new IEEPA findings, separate national-security tariffs under Section 232, or legislation Congress won't pass. The legal reasoning here is solid, rooted in the 1974 trade act's clear limits on presidential power, and the appeals path to the Supreme Court will take months. What's thin is the practical certainty: tariffs remain in effect during appeal, and the administration's workaround strategy is deliberately obscured.

This belongs in TECH because trade architecture is infrastructure. Chip fabs, server hardware, and consumer electronics all move through these tariff schedules. The court's rejection of IEEPA as an all-purpose economic weapon also constrains how future administrations — not just this one — can weaponize emergency statutes against technology supply chains. Worth watching whether Congress now faces pressure to reclaim trade authority it has mostly abandoned.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/trumps-10-global-tariff-is-illegal-court-rules/))

### GM agrees to pay $12.75M in California driver privacy settlement

> **Summary.** General Motors *(Detroit-based automaker, major push into EVs and autonomous driving)* settled with California Attorney General Rob Bonta's office and a coalition of state prosecutors over claims it collected and sold detailed driver behavioral data — acceleration, braking, location, seatbelt use — to insurers and other third parties without adequate consent. The $12.75M is small for GM but sets a marker for automotive privacy enforcement.

The settlement's substance matters more than the dollar figure. Bonta's complaint alleged GM gathered granular telematics through its OnStar system and monetized it through data brokers, with drivers often unaware via buried terms. This is the first major automotive privacy settlement to explicitly name insurance scoring as a harm — your actual driving behavior, sold to insurers who may raise rates. The evidence is strong on collection practices (documented in GM's own partner agreements) and weaker on the downstream insurance harm, which GM disputes. The consent decree requires clearer disclosures and driver opt-in for data sharing, which will ripple to other automakers running similar programs.

For tech readers, this is a regulation event with product-design teeth. Connected cars are privacy nightmares by architecture — dozens of sensors, always-on connectivity, OEM-controlled software stacks. California's enforcement here extends beyond traditional breach-or-consent frameworks into the fairness of the business model itself. Ford, Stellantis, and the Hyundai-Kia group all run comparable data programs; they'll be reading this closely.

([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/09/gm-agrees-to-pay-12-75m-in-california-driver-privacy-settlement/))

### Chaos erupts as cyberattack disrupts learning platform Canvas amid finals

> **Summary.** Instructure's Canvas *(widely-used learning management system, hosts coursework and grades for thousands of K-12 districts and universities)* suffered a cyberattack during peak finals season, forcing schools nationwide to postpone exams and extend deadlines. The company confirmed "unauthorized access" but has not disclosed attack type, extent of data exposure, or whether ransom was involved.

The security story here is the information asymmetry. Schools made rapid operational decisions — postponing tests, going paper-based — while Instructure released minimal technical detail. This is standard incident-response opacity, but it leaves administrators guessing at recovery timelines and students at unknown risk of data exposure. The Ars piece usefully notes Canvas's ubiquity: this isn't a niche EdTech player, it's infrastructure for roughly 6,000 institutions. "Cyberattack" covers everything from DDoS to ransomware to data exfiltration; without knowing which, risk assessment is impossible.

What's strong: the reporting on operational impact, with specific schools named and response protocols described. What's thin and flagged as such: any attribution, technical indicators, or scope of compromised data. Instructure's silence may be legally prudent or may indicate ongoing containment work. For readers: if you have credentials in Canvas-linked systems, standard rotation is warranted; if you're an administrator, the incident underscores how concentrated EdTech supply chains create single points of failure. The timing — finals week — suggests either opportunism or deliberate pressure tactics, but that's speculation until Instructure says more.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/05/chaos-erupts-as-cyberattack-disrupts-learning-platform-canvas-amid-finals/))
