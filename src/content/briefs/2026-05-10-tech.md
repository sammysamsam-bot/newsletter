### Court rules Trump's 10% tariff is just as illegal as the tariff it replaced

> **Summary.** The US Court of International Trade struck down Trump's 10% global tariff, ruling it illegal under the same reasoning that invalidated an earlier 25% tariff scheme. The decision leaves the administration scrambling for alternative legal theories to impose trade levies, with the tech industry particularly exposed to supply-chain disruption. The White House has vowed to appeal.

This is consequential regulation with teeth. The court didn't merely trim the tariff rate—it found the entire statutory basis defective, meaning Trump's promise to reimpose levies "a different way" requires finding a *new* legal hook rather than repackaging the old one. The opinion's reasoning appears solid: the International Emergency Economic Powers Act doesn't grant blank-check tariff authority, and the court's prior ruling on the 25% version foreclosed the obvious workaround. What's thin is the practical near-term impact. The administration will appeal, and Customs and Border Protection may continue collecting duties during litigation. The "tech industry on edge" framing from Ars does real work here—it's plausible but unsourced, and we don't have actual quotes from semiconductor or hardware firms yet. The stronger read is procedural: this constrains executive trade power in a way that will outlast any single tariff fight, and Congress now faces pressure to either authorize or further constrain future levies.

The temporal framing matters too. This drops as trade negotiations with multiple partners hang in limbo; partners who stalled hoping for court relief now have it, potentially hardening their positions. For infrastructure-heavy tech—data center builds, hardware manufacturing, component sourcing—the uncertainty itself is the tax, regardless of whether the 10% ultimately sticks.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/trumps-10-global-tariff-is-illegal-court-rules/))

### GM agrees to pay $12.75M in California driver privacy settlement

> **Summary.** General Motors settled with California and multistate law enforcement over privacy violations tied to its OnStar Smart Driver program, which collected and sold granular driving behavior data to insurers without adequate disclosure. The $12.75M penalty includes a prohibition on sharing such data for five years. GM admits no wrongdoing.

This is the first major US automotive privacy settlement with real money and real operational constraints, not just a consent decree slapped on a vendor no one's heard of. The evidence quality is strong on the investigative side—California AG Rob Bonta's office built the case from consumer complaints and appears to have obtained internal GM communications showing the data flows were more extensive than disclosed. Where it gets thinner is the "without adequate disclosure" framing. The settlement papers aren't public yet, so we're relying on AG press releases and GM's no-fault posture to reconstruct what drivers were actually told. The $12.75M is modest against GM's revenue but not trivial for a privacy line item; it sets a baseline that will figure in every subsequent automotive data negotiation.

The infra-shift angle is what earns this its slot. Connected vehicles have been hoovering behavioral data for years with regulatory lag; this settlement accelerates the compliance clock for every OEM with telematics. The five-year data-sharing ban is the more consequential term than the fine—it's a direct revenue haircut on a business model GM and competitors have treated as found money. Whether other states piggyback or Congress finally moves on comprehensive auto privacy will determine if this is a one-off or a model.

([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/09/gm-agrees-to-pay-12-75m-in-california-driver-privacy-settlement/))

### Fintech startup Parker files for bankruptcy

> **Summary.** Parker *(corporate card and banking services startup, ~$70M raised from firms including Stripe and Joshua Kushner's Thrive Capital)* has filed for bankruptcy and reportedly shut down operations. The collapse follows a rapid 2021-2022 growth phase and subsequent funding environment contraction.

This is the funding-round cluster item, and it's grim in a useful way. Parker's cap table was credible—Stripe, Thrive, YC—and its model (corporate cards for e-commerce brands with revenue-based underwriting) was pitched as the next Brex. The Ars/TechCrunch reporting is thin on proximate cause: was it a bank run by depositors, a regulator action, or simply cash exhaustion? The "widely reported to have shut down" phrasing suggests journalists are relying on secondhand employee social posts rather than official statements, which is fair signal that Parker isn't controlling its own narrative.

What makes this worth including against the cap is the pattern recognition. Parker joins a queue of 2021-vintage fintech startups—Swiftly, Emburse's narrower plays, several BNPL adjuncts—that raised on growth multiples now impossible to justify. The infrastructure shift here is in venture itself: the "well-funded" descriptor has become a warning sign, not a credential. We don't have the bankruptcy filing details yet, so the creditor priority and whether customer funds are at risk remain open. If you're tracking startup mortality as a leading indicator for LP sentiment, Parker is a data point; if you're looking for systemic fintech contagion, we need to see whether their issuing bank partner or card network exposure ripples outward.
