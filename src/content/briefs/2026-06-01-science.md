### STING Protein "Switch" Drives Alzheimer's Inflammation

> **Summary.** Scripps Research scientists identified a chemical alteration in the STING *(stimulator of interferon genes)* protein that locks brain immune cells into chronic overdrive in Alzheimer's disease. The finding points to a druggable mechanism for halting the neuroinflammation that damages synapses, rather than merely treating symptoms.

The study argues that Alzheimer's progression involves more than amyloid plaques and tau tangles—that a sustained inflammatory response becomes its own driver of harm. The evidence centers on post-mortem brain tissue showing hyperactivated STING in microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, plus experiments where blocking the pathway protected synaptic connections in models. This is strong mechanistic work: STING is a well-characterized innate immunity node already targeted by several biotechs for autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, so there's a translational road map. What's thinner is clinical relevance—we don't know yet whether STING inhibition in humans would slow cognitive decline, or if the inflammation is by that point irreversibly downstream of other damage.

Still, this reframes Alzheimer's as partly an immune dysregulation disease, which would be a genuine practice shift if trials follow. The field's recent antibody successes *(lecanemab, donanemab)* have been modest and side-effect-laden; a small-molecule approach with better access to brain tissue would be welcome.

### Classical Computing Solves Chemistry Problem Long Assumed to Need Quantum Machines

> **Summary.** A decades-in-the-making proof shows that ordinary classical computers can fully characterize a class of complex chemical reactions previously thought to require quantum computation. The result undercuts a major theoretical justification for near-term quantum chemistry applications.

Quanta's reporting on this is clear about what's been settled: a specific problem in *electronic structure theory*—determining ground-state energies of certain interacting systems—turns out to have an efficient classical algorithm after all. The researchers exploited hidden mathematical structure that prior work missed. This is methodology-shifting for computational chemistry, where funding and research direction have increasingly assumed quantum advantage was necessary for progress. The evidence here is a mathematical proof, so it's rigorous rather than empirical, though the practical algorithms need implementation and benchmarking against existing approximations.

What's honestly uncertain: whether this collapses *all* the chemistry problems quantum computing advocates care about, or just this class. IBM, Google, and startups like PsiQuantum have bet heavily on quantum chemistry as an early application. This doesn't kill that bet, but it narrows the justification to problems this proof doesn't cover. We'll want to see which chemists adopt the new algorithms and where they outperform density functional theory or coupled-cluster methods in actual calculations.

([Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/key-chemistry-question-answered-no-quantum-computer-required-20260529/))

### 3D Stacked Silicon Process Targets Real Chip Production, Not Just Lab Wafers

> **Summary.** Researchers developed a low-temperature process using ultra-thin silicon membranes to build true three-dimensional integrated circuits, overcoming thermal damage that has blocked vertical stacking. The work explicitly targets extending Moore's Law *(the roughly two-year doubling of transistor density)* as planar scaling slows.

The breakthrough here is manufacturing viability: previous 3D approaches required high-temperature steps that degraded lower-layer circuitry, limiting stacking to two layers or specialized applications like memory. This group's process stays cool enough for logic transistors throughout the stack. ScienceDaily's summary is light on specifics—node target, demonstrated layer count, yield numbers—so we're trusting the paper's claims about scalability. The source is a university press release, not an industry foundry announcement, which matters: we've seen promising lab processes fail on throughput or defect rates.

That said, the problem is real and well-defined. TSMC, Intel, and Samsung are all pursuing some form of 3D integration *(hybrid bonding, sequential fabrication, chiplet stacking)*. A lower-temperature generic process would be broadly adoptable if it meets electrical and economic specs. What's needed next: a foundry partner to pilot, or at least independent electrical characterization showing competitive performance versus existing 2.5D and 3D options.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260530053412.htm))
