### Superconductivity record falls at normal pressure

> **Summary.** University of Houston researchers hit 151 Kelvin *(−122°C, or about −188°F)* for zero-resistance electrical conduction without extreme pressure apparatus, breaking a 30-year-old ceiling. The advance matters practically because prior high-temperature superconductors often required crushing pressures (think diamond-anvil cells) that make scaling impossible; this result moves the boundary toward something engineers could actually build around.

The framing here is materials science with a path toward application, not just a physics milestone. The previous record-holder, a thallium-based compound from the early 1990s, topped out near 138 K at ambient pressure—so 151 K is a meaningful jump, not statistical noise. What's strong: the group used established synthesis methods (solid-state reaction, standard ceramic processing) rather than exotic fabrication, which suggests reproducibility is plausible. What's thin: the ScienceDaily writeup gives no resistance-temperature curves, no mention of critical current density *(how much current the material can carry before superconductivity collapses)*, and no structural characterization. Those are the variables that determine whether this lives in a lab or eventually wires a power grid. We want to see the preprint or peer-reviewed paper before treating the claim as settled.

The 30-year stagnation in this specific category isn't for lack of trying—room-temperature ambient-pressure superconductivity remains the holy grail, with the LK-99 fiasco *(2023 claim of room-temperature superconduction in a copper-substituted lead apatite, later debunked)* still fresh memory. That episode trained healthy skepticism on this entire subfield. This team's prior work in high-pressure hydrides gives them credibility, but credibility isn't confirmation. The honest read: promising, physics-plausible, requires independent replication and engineering-relevant metrics.

### Parkinson's spread mechanism blocked in early experiments

> **Summary.** Researchers identified GPNMB *(glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B, previously known in other disease contexts)* as a protein released by immune cells that appears to ferry toxic α-synuclein aggregates between neurons, accelerating Parkinson's progression. Antibody blockade stopped this cell-to-cell transmission in experimental models, opening a mechanistically novel therapeutic angle distinct from dopamine replacement.

This is a methodology-shifting finding if it holds. The dominant narrative in Parkinson's progression has been template-directed misfolding—bad protein touches good protein, good protein goes bad, repeat—but the *how* of spatial spread remained murky. GPNMB offers a transport vehicle: immune cells (microglia, likely) respond to neuronal damage by secreting this protein, which binds misfolded α-synuclein and effectively delivers it to neighboring cells. That reframes microglia from passive cleanup crew to active disease accelerator, which has implications for when and how to intervene.

The evidence quality is mixed in instructive ways. Strong: the mechanistic story is coherent and testable—knockout and antibody experiments show reduced spread. Thin: "early experiments" typically means cell culture and possibly mouse models; human brain tissue validation and longitudinal data aren't mentioned here. The α-synuclein spreading hypothesis itself has faced replication challenges, with some groups arguing the observed pathology progression reflects independent regional vulnerability rather than actual prion-like propagation. This GPNMB finding doesn't resolve that debate; it complicates it productively. The honest hedge: we're at "biologically plausible mechanism with preliminary intervention evidence," not "disease-modifying therapy incoming." But in a field starved of either, that's worth tracking closely.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260527023214.htm))

### Long COVID's brain signature isn't what researchers expected

> **Summary.** A brain imaging study found no widespread neuroinflammation in long COVID patients; instead, severe symptoms correlated with hyperactivity in mood- and emotion-processing regions. The finding challenges the autoimmune/inflammation hypothesis that has driven much therapeutic trial design and redirects attention toward dysregulation in functional brain networks.

This is a practice-influencing result with real methodological weight. The inflammation model has been dominant enough that trials of anti-inflammatories (colchicine, low-dose naltrexone, various cytokine inhibitors) are ongoing or recently reported. If the imaging holds up, those trials may be targeting the wrong biology—expensive misspent effort in a condition affecting millions. The study's design strength is worth noting: it used PET imaging *(positron emission tomography, measuring metabolic activity and tracer uptake)* rather than relying on peripheral inflammatory markers or post-hoc symptom correlation, so it's actually looking at brain tissue rather than inferring from blood draws.

What's strong: the negative finding on neuroinflammation is concrete and falsifiable; positive claims of absence are harder to spin than presence. What's thin: long COVID is almost certainly heterogeneous—this may be true for a subtype rather than the whole syndrome, and the mood-region hyperactivity could itself be secondary to systemic inflammation rather than a primary brain disorder. The ScienceDaily summary doesn't clarify whether the hyperactivity normalized with recovery or correlated with specific symptom clusters, both of which would sharpen interpretation. Still, the reframing has immediate implications: clinicians managing long COVID may need to reconsider whether they're treating an inflammatory disease, and researchers should be designing studies that can parse subtypes rather than assuming uniformity. The honest take: this doesn't solve long COVID, but it may prevent us from solving the wrong version of it.
