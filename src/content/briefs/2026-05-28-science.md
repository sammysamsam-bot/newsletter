### Blocking a Protein That Fuels Parkinson's Spread

> **Summary.** Researchers identified GPNMB, a protein released by immune cells in response to damaged neurons, as a driver of Parkinson's disease progression through the brain. Antibodies blocking GPNMB stopped the toxic cell-to-cell spread in early experiments. The finding opens a new intervention point against the disease's relentless advance, not just its symptoms.

The study positions GPNMB as the missing mechanistic link in how Parkinson's propagates: damaged neurons trigger immune cells, which release this protein, which then accelerates further degeneration. That's a coherent cascade with clear therapeutic implications. The evidence so far is preclinical—cell cultures and animal models—and the press release doesn't specify which antibody format or delivery route. What's strong is the biological plausibility; neuroinflammation has long been suspected in Parkinson's progression, and pinning a specific protein to the process gives drug developers a target. What's thin is any timeline for human trials. The antibody approach also raises the standard CNS drug-development concerns: getting large molecules across the blood-brain barrier remains expensive and failure-prone. This is a genuine advance in understanding, not yet a practice-changer.

### Superconductivity Record Broken at Normal Pressure

> **Summary.** University of Houston researchers achieved superconductivity at 151 Kelvin *(−122°C)* under ambient pressure, surpassing a 30-year-old record. The material operates without the extreme pressures that have limited practical application of recent high-temperature superconductors. The advance inches the field closer to the long-sought goal of room-temperature superconductivity.

This is methodology-shifting in a field crowded with vaporware. The 1986 Bednorz-Müller discovery of cuprate superconductors set off decades of pursuit; subsequent "breakthroughs" often collapsed under replication failure *(see: LK-99, 2023)*. What's notable here is the normal-pressure condition—previous records near this temperature required diamond-anvil cells at crushing pressures, making them laboratory curiosities. The Houston team hasn't yet disclosed the material's composition, which is frustrating but not unusual for a group likely filing patents. We should flag that ScienceDaily is reprising a university press release; independent measurement and peer review are still outstanding. The 151 K figure matters because it crosses a practical threshold: liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K, so this remains cryogenic, but ambient-pressure cooling is vastly cheaper than megabar pressure cells. If it holds, power transmission and MRI magnets could eventually benefit. If.

### Long COVID's Brain Signature Isn't Inflammation After All

> **Summary.** A brain imaging study found no widespread neuroinflammation in long COVID patients; instead, severe symptoms correlated with heightened activity in mood- and emotion-processing regions. The result challenges a dominant explanatory model and suggests the condition's neurological dimension may be more functional than structural. Implications for treatment targeting are significant if the finding replicates.

This is the kind of methodology-shifting negative result that deserves attention precisely because it contradicts a settled narrative. The neuroinflammation hypothesis has driven considerable research funding and trial design; these scans suggest researchers may have been hunting in the wrong tissue compartment. The study design isn't fully detailed in the press release—we don't know the imaging modality *(likely PET with TSPO tracers or similar)*, sample size, or whether patients were stratified by symptom cluster. What's strong is the directness: they looked for a signal that was supposed to be there and found absence instead, plus an unexpected correlation elsewhere. The mood-region hyperactivity could imply a different therapeutic target—neuromodulation, targeted psychiatry, or rehabilitation approaches rather than immunomodulators. What's thin is whether this activity is causal or compensatory, and whether it distinguishes long COVID from other post-viral syndromes or depressive states. Replication with larger, more diverse cohorts is essential before clinical practice shifts.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260527023206.htm))
