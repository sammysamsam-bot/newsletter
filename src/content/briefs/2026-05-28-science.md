### Superconductivity record falls — at normal pressure, no less

> **Summary.** A University of Houston team pushed zero-resistance conduction to 151 Kelvin *(−122°C)* under ordinary pressure, breaking a 30-year ceiling. The material is still cryogenic by everyday standards, but bypassing the crushing pressure requirements that plagued earlier "breakthroughs" is a genuine methodology shift. Whether the compound can be scaled or wired into devices remains the open question.

The 1980s ceramic superconductors topped out around 133 K, and recent room-temperature claims have collapsed under scrutiny or required pressures found only in diamond anvils. This result lands in a narrow useful zone: liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K, so 151 K is still far from consumer electronics, yet close enough that engineering a cooling chain is plausible rather than absurd. The Houston group's paper — we've only got the press release so far — claims the record was achieved in a bulk material, not a microscale flake, which matters for whether anyone else can reproduce it. The 30-year stagnation in this particular bracket suggests the advance is real; the history of the field suggests we wait for independent replication before revising any roadmaps.

What we'd want to see next: the chemical formula, crystal structure, and whether critical current density *(how much current the material can carry before superconductivity collapses)* holds at anything like useful values. High-temperature superconductors have a habit of shattering under magnetic fields or current loads. Still, normal-pressure operation removes one enormous barrier. If the material is cheap and the fabrication repeatable, this could eventually touch MRI magnets, grid storage, and fusion confinement — the usual suspects, but with one fewer fantasy requirement.

### Depression treatment pivots from brain chemistry to immune quieting

> **Summary.** A small clinical trial repurposed an anti-inflammatory already used for rheumatoid arthritis *(likely a TNF-alpha inhibitor or similar cytokine blocker; the release doesn't name the drug)* for treatment-resistant depression. Patients showed symptom relief plus downstream gains in fatigue, anxiety, and quality of life — not the selective mood-only pattern you'd expect from placebo or expectancy effects.

The framing here is "surprising" — neuroscience PR's favorite word — but the immunology-depression link has been building for fifteen years, from post-interferon depression to elevated CRP in suicidality. What's genuinely shifted is that someone ran a proper randomized trial with psychiatric endpoints rather than just correlational biomarker studies. The release is frustratingly thin on numbers: no N, no effect size, no comparison to active placebo or even standard antidepressants. "Small clinical trial" could mean 20 patients or 120; the difference matters for whether we're looking at a pilot signal or something approaching registrational evidence.

The mechanism, if it holds, would expand the depression target from monoamines to peripheral inflammation, with implications for comorbid cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and perhaps the long-COVID psychiatric sequelae below. We'd want to know: did anti-inflammatory effects precede mood improvements? Was there a dose-response? And what happens at week 12, when TNF blockers in RA start showing infection risks? The rheumatology safety profile is well-characterized, which speeds translation if the efficacy replicates. That's the real promise here — repurposing known drugs short-circuits the decade-long development pipeline. But known drugs also have known failures; infliximab flopped in a larger depression trial circa 2013. This one may be different by design (treatment-resistant population, different agent) or it may be noise. The data, not the narrative, will tell.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260527023204.htm))

### Parkinson's propagation tied to immune protein — and blocked in early work

> **Summary.** Researchers identified GPNMB *(glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B, a known immune modulator)* as a vehicle for Parkinson's pathology spreading between neurons. In cell and animal experiments, antibodies against GPNMB interrupted the transmission chain. The finding gives a specific molecular handle on a process previously described only in vague "prion-like" terms.

The alpha-synuclein spreading hypothesis — that misfolded protein moves from cell to cell like an infection, templating further misfolding — has dominated Parkinson's research for a decade, yet produced no therapies. GPNMB's role as the shuttle, released by stressed microglia and taken up by neurons, is a mechanistic advance with clear therapeutic implications: block the shuttle, not just the cargo. The early antibody work is promising but thin — "stopped the toxic process from spreading between cells" likely means cultured neurons or mouse slices, not living patients. The press release doesn't specify model or readout.

What's strong: GPNMB was identified through unbiased proteomics in patient tissue, not cherry-picked from a hypothesis. That reduces prior-commitment bias. What's thin: the therapeutic leap from "antibody works in a dish" to " slows human neurodegeneration" is the same chasm that has swallowed every other alpha-synuclein-targeted therapy. Genentech's semorinemab failed phase 2 in 2022; Biogen's cinpanemab was worse. The field has learned that clearing synuclein doesn't clearly help if the spreading mechanism remains intact. GPNMB targeting is theoretically cleaner — interrupt transmission rather than mop up already-formed aggregates — but the clinical history counsels humility. We'd watch for whether the academic group or a partnered biotech advances toward IND-enabling studies, and whether GPNMB expression correlates with disease progression in human cohorts.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260527023214.htm))
