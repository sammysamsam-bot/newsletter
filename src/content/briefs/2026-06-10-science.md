### A common joint supplement may accelerate Alzheimer's decline

> **Summary.** A major observational study links glucosamine use to roughly 25% faster progression from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's dementia, with biological clues suggesting the supplement may interfere with brain glucose metabolism. The finding, if replicated, would upend a staple of the $50 billion supplement aisle.

The study's strength is its scale and the mechanistic breadcrumbs: glucosamine appears to compete with glucose in neurons, starving them over time. That's a coherent story, not just a correlation. But it's still observational — people taking glucosamine may differ in unmeasured ways, and the 25% figure comes with confidence intervals we should see before treating it as precise. The biological mechanism makes this worth flagging now, not waiting for a randomized trial that may never come (who'd fund blinding people to a cheap supplement?). The honest read: plausible signal, strong enough that I'd pause before recommending glucosamine to anyone with cognitive concerns, but not yet settled science.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260610003044.htm))

### The largest opioid review finds they often fail for acute pain

> **Summary.** A systematic review of acute-pain opioid trials — the largest conducted — finds the drugs frequently no better than placebo for common conditions including kidney stones and post-surgical pain, with dependence risk detectable after short use. The findings challenge decades of prescribing norms.

This is methodology-shifting in how we weigh evidence in pain medicine. The review's scale matters: previous meta-analyses were smaller and often industry-influenced. The "no better than placebo" finding for specific conditions is stark, though we need to know which surgeries and which dosing regimens — the raw summary smooths over heterogeneity. The dependence finding after short use is well-supported elsewhere and gains force here. Limitation: placebo response in acute pain trials is notoriously high, so "no better than placebo" doesn't mean patients felt nothing; it means opioids didn't outperform a pill with no active drug. Still, given side-effect burden and addiction risk, that's enough to ask whether opioids should remain first-line.

([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260608040023.htm))

### Home exercise beats "chemo brain" in trial

> **Summary.** A randomized trial of cancer patients found a simple home-based exercise program improved attention and reduced self-reported cognitive problems compared to placebo control, while low-dose ibuprofen showed weaker, less consistent benefits. Up to 80% of chemotherapy patients experience "chemo brain."

The exercise intervention wins here because it's human RCT data on a genuinely disabling problem with few proven treatments. The attention measures were objective; the cognitive-problems report was subjective — both moved in the same direction, which is reassuring. We don't know which exercise components mattered (aerobic? resistance? scheduling?), or whether effects persist beyond the trial window. The ibuprofen arm is a sideshow: nominally positive on some measures, inconsistent, and adding NSAID risks to chemotherapy toxicity seems unappealing. The real signal is that structured movement, even modest and at home, appears to protect cognition during treatment. Oncology practices should pay attention; implementation looks low-cost and low-risk.
