### NVIDIA ships Cosmos 3, an open omni-model for physical AI

> **Summary.** NVIDIA *(semiconductor and AI infrastructure company)* released Cosmos 3, an open "omni-model" for physical AI reasoning and action. It is built to handle multimodal understanding in physical-world contexts — robotics, autonomous systems, embodied AI — and ships with open weights via Hugging Face. The model architecture aims to unify perception, reasoning, and action prediction under one checkpoint, a departure from the pipeline approach that dominates robotics today.

NVIDIA's positioning here is strategic as much as technical. Cosmos 3 arrives as the company faces antitrust scrutiny in China, the EU, and from the US DOJ, with its $20 billion "not-acqui-hire" of Shamrock Holdings still making headlines. An open-weights play in embodied AI builds developer goodwill and seeds an ecosystem on NVIDIA hardware before competitors can establish alternatives. The omni-model claim is ambitious: most robotics stacks today chain separate models for vision, language, planning, and control. Whether Cosmos 3 actually outperforms those pipelines on real robots — versus curated benchmarks — is the gap to watch. The Hugging Face release includes inference code but no training data disclosure, so reproducibility will depend on community validation.

The licensing matters too. "Open" in NVIDIA's vocabulary has historically meant weights-available, not training-reproducible or commercially unrestricted. Developers should check the license terms before building products on it.

([Hugging Face Blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/nvidia/cosmos-3-for-physical-ai))

### Anthropic details Claude sandboxing across products

> **Summary.** Anthropic published a first-party breakdown of how it sandboxes Claude across Claude.ai, Claude Code, and Cowork *(its enterprise collaborative product)*. The post describes layered boundaries: process sandboxes, VMs, filesystem restrictions, and egress controls designed to prevent credential exfiltration and limit blast radius if an agent is compromised.

This is the kind of documentation Simon Willison — and we — have been asking for. The post is unusually specific about implementation: credentials are kept out of the sandbox, filesystem access is scoped, and network egress is filtered. What it doesn't quantify is how these controls fare against adversarial prompting or whether they've been independently audited. The framing is "here is what we built" rather than "here is what has been tested and verified." Still, in an industry where agent security is mostly vendor hand-waving, Anthropic's transparency sets a baseline others will be measured against. The timing is notable too: as agents gain tool-use capabilities and longer-running execution, the attack surface expands dramatically. Anthropic is essentially pre-empting the "but what happens when Claude goes rogue?" question with architecture rather than assurances.

([Anthropic via Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/30/how-we-contain-claude/))

### OpenAI ships GPT-5.5 to enterprise; Boston Children's and Braintrust deploy

> **Summary.** OpenAI rolled out GPT-5.5 with two enterprise case studies: Boston Children's Hospital using it to diagnose over 40 rare disease cases, and Braintrust *(AI evaluation and experimentation platform)* using Codex — OpenAI's coding agent — to turn customer requests into production code. Both deployments emphasize operational integration rather than research benchmarks.

The Boston Children's claim is the stronger signal: 40+ rare disease diagnoses assisted by AI, presumably via pattern matching across fragmented medical literature and patient records. What we don't know: the false positive rate, whether diagnoses were confirmed by standard-of-care methods, and whether the hospital has FDA clearance or is operating under research protocols. Rare diseases are a sensible AI application — data is scarce, specialists are few, and the cost of missing a diagnosis is high — but "helped diagnose" is a weasel phrase until we see the clinical validation.

The Braintrust case is more conventional: AI-assisted coding for an AI tooling company. Dogfooding, in other words. The Codex integration apparently handles experiment-running and code generation from customer requests, which is useful but not a dramatic capability advance. Clustering these together because they're the same GPT-5.5 rollout wave; neither justifies its own slot.

([OpenAI News — Boston Children's](https://openai.com/index/boston-childrens-hospital) · [OpenAI News — Braintrust](https://openai.com/index/braintrust))

### SoftBank commits €75 billion for French data centers

> **Summary.** SoftBank *(Japanese multinational investment holding company)* announced plans to invest up to €75 billion to build and operate French data centers, targeting 5 gigawatts of additional capacity. The deal represents one of the largest data center commitments in Europe and positions France as a counterweight to the concentrated AI infrastructure buildout in the US and GCC states.

This is infrastructure-as-geopolitics. France has been aggressively courting AI investment through nuclear-powered energy guarantees and EU subsidy alignment, and SoftBank — which holds a massive stake in Arm and has pivoted hard toward AI through its Vision Fund — is the natural partner. The 5GW figure is substantial: roughly equivalent to several nuclear reactors' output, and far beyond current French data center capacity. What remains unclear is the timeline, the actual cash versus announced commitment, and whether this displaces previously planned UK or German investments. Masayoshi Son's announcement-to-delivery ratio has been uneven; the €75 billion headline should be treated as intent, not spend. Still, the sheer scale ensures it will reshape European cloud and training-cluster geography if even half-built.

([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/30/softbank-says-it-will-invest-up-to-e75-billion-to-build-french-data-centers/))

### GitHub Copilot switches to token-based billing; developer pushback

> **Summary.** GitHub Copilot moved from flat-rate subscription pricing to token-based billing, charging developers per token consumed rather than per month. The change has prompted significant developer consternation, with many reporting that unpredictable costs are replacing predictable overhead — particularly for teams running large context windows or iterative coding sessions.

This is a meaningful product shift dressed as pricing optimization. Token-based billing aligns GitHub's revenue with actual model inference costs, which have risen as models get larger and context windows expand. But it breaks the implicit contract that made Copilot attractive: fixed-cost coding assistance. For developers using AI for exploration and iteration — the "write a quick script, one hour later" workflow — costs now scale with experimentation, which is exactly when AI is most useful. The framing from GitHub emphasizes "fairness" and "pay for what you use," but the predictable effect is usage anxiety and shorter context windows. Whether this pushes developers toward open-weight local models (Ollama, etc.) or competing flat-rate tools is the dynamic to track. The "golden age" rhetoric in TechCrunch's headline is overheated, but the underlying tension — inference costs must flow to someone — is real.

([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/30/what-a-joke-github-copilots-new-token-based-billing-spurs-consternation-among-devs/))
