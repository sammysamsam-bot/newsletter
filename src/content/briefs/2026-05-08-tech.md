### Google relents on AI Overviews sourcing after publisher pressure

> **Summary.** Google will add more prominent source links to AI Overviews, including direct citations and a new "sources" button showing referenced webpages. The concession follows months of complaints from publishers whose traffic collapsed as Google kept answers inside its own interface. The change rolls out "in the coming weeks" for English queries in the US.

Google's post frames this as proactive improvement; the timeline suggests otherwise. Publishers have been documenting traffic erosion since Overviews launched broadly in May 2024, and antitrust pressure in the US and EU has mounted simultaneously. The actual mechanism matters: "sources" buttons have historically been ignored by users, while the underlying incentive structure — Google monetizing content without sending visitors — remains intact. The company says it's "exploring" revenue-sharing with publishers, which is the real fight. This is a tactical retreat, not a structural fix.

What we'd need to see: whether referral traffic actually recovers post-change, and whether Google commits to anything beyond voluntary gestures. The DOJ's ongoing search remedies trial could force more binding terms.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/google/2026/05/google-will-put-more-links-to-websites-in-ai-overviews/))

### Court rules Trump's 10% tariff illegal — trade policy reset ahead

> **Summary.** The US Court of International Trade struck down Trump's across-the-board 10% tariff, ruling the administration lacked authority to impose it under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. The decision mirrors an earlier ruling against the April "Liberation Day" tariffs and tees up Supreme Court review.

Two consecutive federal court losses on the same statutory theory suggest the administration's trade strategy has a serious legal foundation problem. The White House has already appealed, and the circuit split essentially guarantees Supreme Court attention. For tech specifically: hardware pricing uncertainty continues, and the "different way" Trump promised — presumably sector-specific tariffs under other authorities — will face similar scrutiny. The article's framing around tech industry anxiety is slightly overplayed; the immediate issue is legal process, not policy substance.

What's strong: the court's reasoning on IEEPA scope, which draws a bright line between emergency response and permanent economic restructuring. What's thin: speculation about what "different way" means in practice.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/05/trumps-10-global-tariff-is-illegal-court-rules/))

### Mozilla deploys AI bug-hunter Mythos across Firefox codebase

> **Summary.** Mozilla found 271 security vulnerabilities using Mythos *(AI-assisted static analysis tool)* across Firefox and related projects, with "almost no false positives" per the company's security team. The findings include 74 high-severity bugs, and Mozilla says it has "completely bought in" on AI-assisted discovery.

This is a genuine operational shift, not a pilot. Mozilla's security team is credible and historically conservative about tooling claims. The "almost no false positives" assertion is notable — most static analysis tools trade coverage for noise — and suggests Mythos is doing something different in its abstraction layer modeling. That said, we don't have independent verification or details on which bug classes Mythos catches versus misses. The real test is whether this scales to other large codebases without Mozilla's specific architecture.

The broader pattern: AI coding assistants generate bugs faster than humans can find them, so AI-assisted discovery becomes table stakes. Mozilla's early adoption here is strategically significant for a browser that competes on security positioning.

([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2026/05/mozilla-says-271-vulnerabilities-found-by-mythos-have-almost-no-false-positives/))
